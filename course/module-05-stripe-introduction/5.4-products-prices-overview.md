# 5.4 — Products & Prices Overview

> The two-object model that catches 90% of new Stripe users off guard.

## Why this matters

A Product and a Price are two separate Stripe objects. "Plus plan" is a Product. "$10 per month in USD" is a Price. "$100 per year in USD" is a *different* Price attached to the same Product. This split feels over-engineered until the first time you change an annual from $100 to $120 without breaking existing monthly subscribers — at which point you nod sagely.

We're going to model **3 products × 2 prices = 6 prices** for Contactly:

| Product | Prices |
|---|---|
| Free | (no paid price) |
| Plus | $10/month, $96/year |
| Pro | $20/month, $192/year |

## The Principal Engineer lens

**Prices are immutable.** Once a Price object has an amount or interval attached, you can't change it — you deactivate it and create a new one. This forces you to version your pricing history: a customer on the old price keeps it; a new customer gets the new one. Stripe enforces this discipline for you; embrace it.

Corollary: **don't hardcode price IDs in your code.** IDs change every time you create a new Price. Instead, assign a stable **lookup key** to each Price and resolve to ID at runtime. (We'll cover lookup keys in 5.6.)

## Step 1 — Free ≠ No Product

You might think "the free tier doesn't need a product in Stripe." For our app that's fine — free users never interact with Stripe. But some apps create a $0 Price for Free so the subscription object always exists. We'll keep Free outside of Stripe entirely; simpler, fewer corner cases.

## Step 2 — Recurring vs. one-time

Every Price has a `type`: `recurring` or `one_time`. We use `recurring` for Plus/Pro. If we ever sold a "one-time credit pack," we'd use `one_time`. Don't mix these — a Subscription only accepts recurring prices.

## Step 3 — Intervals & interval counts

A recurring price has:

- `interval`: `day` | `week` | `month` | `year`.
- `interval_count`: number of intervals (e.g., `interval: 'month', interval_count: 3` = every 3 months).

We only need `month` (count 1) and `year` (count 1). Most apps only ever use these two.

## Step 4 — Currency matters

Prices are per-currency. `$10 USD/month` and `€9 EUR/month` are two Price objects. Contactly will be USD-only to start (Module 13 talks about how you'd add more).

## Step 5 — Tax behavior

Stripe supports automatic tax calculation. The field `tax_behavior` on a Price can be `exclusive`, `inclusive`, or `unspecified`. For simplicity we'll leave tax off in this course (set `tax_behavior` to `exclusive` implicitly). If you ship in the EU you'll want Stripe Tax on; that's a Module-12-in-production decision.

## Step 6 — Proration: Stripe's killer feature

When a user upgrades mid-cycle from Plus-monthly ($10) to Pro-monthly ($20), Stripe auto-calculates the prorated charge: they pay the $10 difference × fraction-of-period-remaining. You don't write a line of math. You just call `subscriptions.update()` with the new price ID; Stripe handles the rest.

This is why you want Stripe to be the source of truth. Re-implementing proration in your DB would be a months-long project, buggy forever.

## Step 7 — What to store in your DB

From Stripe, we'll mirror the *minimum* subset we need:

| Object | Field | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Product | `id`, `name`, `active` | Render the pricing page. |
| Price | `id`, `product_id`, `unit_amount`, `currency`, `interval`, `lookup_key` | Know "what's the ID for Plus-monthly?" |
| Customer | `id`, `user_id` | Link Stripe customer → our user. |
| Subscription | `id`, `user_id`, `status`, `current_period_end`, `price_id` | Entitlement checks. |

We won't mirror: product descriptions (read from Stripe live), payment method details (never touch them), invoice line items (Stripe has a hosted invoice URL).

## Step 8 — The purchase flow at 10k feet

Here's the end-to-end dance we're building toward in Modules 6–9:

1. User clicks "Upgrade to Plus" → our server creates a Checkout Session with the Plus-monthly Price ID.
2. User is redirected to Stripe's hosted checkout → enters card details → pays.
3. Stripe redirects back to our `/billing/success` page.
4. Meanwhile, Stripe fires `checkout.session.completed` + `customer.subscription.created` webhooks to our handler.
5. Our handler mirrors the Customer + Subscription into our DB.
6. The user's app refreshes, sees the new subscription, unlocks the Plus features.

Every arrow in that list is code we'll write. The dashboard just *watches* the dance.

## Common traps

- **Creating 50 Price objects** by accident because you kept tweaking amounts. Archive them as you go; otherwise your dashboard becomes a junkyard.
- **Hardcoding `price_1Nq...` in code.** Breaks the moment you regenerate or migrate environments. Lookup keys only.
- **Expecting to edit a Price amount in place.** You can't. Archive + new Price is the only path.

## Recap

Products describe what you sell; Prices describe how much and how often. They're separate because pricing changes over time and Stripe keeps the history for you.
